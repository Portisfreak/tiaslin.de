Deliver Us from Evil · A Dark Fantasy Campaign

A Dark Fantasy Campaign

Deliver Us
from Evil

Before the light, there was Silence, the age of darkness and despair. A savage age, marked by chaos and disorder, and civilization lay barren and bare. False gods reigned, we sacrificed our blood and souls to them, and were left in dirt and mud, no light to warm our hearts.

A light whispered in the dark and silent. Yet so quiet, our torn hearts could not hear, but for one that wasn't broken.

From the dark rose our savior, our Lady Lucienne, herald of Light, and turned whispers into shouts and cries for us to listen. She broke the silence and unveiled the false gods' lies. Their dark reign should end, and in their place the One True Light would shine.

And thus dawned the age of light. Where there was dirt now orchards flower, cities sprawl, and where there was chaos, our Empire rose to protect what we have nurtured.

Still, the dark prevails. Through witches' whispers, the false gods plot and scheme to douse the light that blinds them. Goblins cross the marches as their faithful servants to announce our desolation.

Oh Lady Lucienne, deliver us from evil. Keep with us the one true light, so we may illuminate the dark that lingers.

The Campaign

The campaign world is a high medieval gothic fantasy setting.

Within the vast and powerful Valoran Empire, your journeys start in Hartmoor, a small barony within the Duchy of Arvannes, not too far from the border provinces of the Empire. Though not yet directly threatened by Goblin Attacks, the looming threat of war and violence sows unease across the people of the duchy, and Duchess Éloane of Arvannes must strive for Empire's support within the High Council of Aubescourt.

Meanwhile, another threat appears to be emerging in the Barony of Hartmoor: rumor speaks of a Covenant cult operating in the region, allegedly responsible for recent heresies and, quite possibly, for a string of murders that were recently committed.

This is your home, and your home is threatened by forces beyond your understanding. With the Goblin armies crossing the marches and false gods' servants looming over your community, the burden falls to you to stand fast and hold the line.

The Powers That Be

The Valoran Empire

Half the known continent, one crown. The Valoran Empire is the world's great success story: roads that run safe, walled towns, guilds and granaries, a fleet that rules its coastline. Its signature is order — the pax imperialis, a peace so real its people have forgotten it was ever otherwise. The imperial crown sits atop a feudal ladder of kings, dukes, and barons, all watched over by the great institutions of the High Council. Beyond the borders, the world is wilder: kingdoms where brigands roam and goblins still raid, and where the Empire's peace ends, its self-image begins.

The Goblin Tribes

Not bandits, not beasts — a people. The goblin tribes have sprawled across the Weald, an unmapped region beyond the marches, since before the Empire, kept survivable for centuries only by their endless feuds with one another. Now the tribes are uniting under a shaman-prophet's banner, settling the marches, and marching to war. Servants of the old gods, they do not come to conquer. They come to end the world of man.

The Dawnward

The Church of the One True Light, founded by the Lady Lucienne herself three centuries ago. It is the soul of the Empire: crowns the emperor, blesses the harvest, tends the poor through its Almoners — and hunts heresy through its Inquisition, for within the Empire the old faith is not only sin, but crime. Its priests carry the Divine, the only sanctioned magic in the world of man. For most folk, the Dawnward is goodness — and most of its faithful genuinely are.

The Covenant

The old faith — the pantheon that reigned during "the Silence". Within the Empire, its practice is outlawed and its name is a curse: witchcraft, devil worship, blood sacrifice. Yet, the old faith has survived: Household rites, oaths, seasonal feasts, and, in some communities, small covenants and sects are secretly practising the old rites where the Dawnward's eye can't reach.

How to Play This World

Faith is everywhere. Even the skeptic swears by the Dawnward when the wolves are at the door. Choose your relationship to it: devout, dutiful, doubting, or daring — but have one.

Magic is not casual. Miracles belong to the Church; everything else is a crime or a secret. Access is limited from the get go, and more often than not, magic comes as a threat rather than a savior.

The world is small and dark. It's as much grimdark as dark fantasy. It's dangerous and unforgiving; behind each smile might loom a hidden knife.

Emphasis

Mystery & Investigation●●●●Clues, interrogations, archives, secrets that have been waiting a long time
Roleplay & Drama●●●●Belief under pressure: what your characters trust, and what it takes to break that trust

Player Options

Ancestries

Work in Progress

The population of the realms of this world is mostly human. Most folk in the countryside live and die never meeting anyone who is not. Those of other blood are few, scattered, and never quite trusted. The following peoples are open to players.

Note: We adapt the modern D&D concept of increasing one Ability Score of your choice by 2, and one Ability Score of your choice by 1, during character creation — doing away with ancestry-related ability scores.

Human

Humans are the people of the world — its villages, its armies, its churches, its heresies. They come in every condition: peasant and prince, zealot and skeptic, saint and knife.

A human can pass anywhere without remark. In a world where difference draws suspicion, that invisibility is worth more than it sounds.

Humanoid · Medium (about 5–7 ft. tall) · Speed 30 ft.

Skillful

You gain proficiency in one skill of your choice.

Versatile

You gain an Origin feat of your choice.

Dwarf

Dwarves are a short, broad, long-lived mountain folk, rarely seen and little known. They keep to their holds in the high peaks; those who walk among other peoples are usually traders or smiths, doing quiet business before vanishing again.

However, some Dwarves made their homes about the towns and cities of the Empire, looking for fortune beyond the mountain dark. They are known as patient, private, and slow to befriend.

Humanoid · Medium (about 4–5 ft. tall) · Speed 30 ft.

Dwarven Resilience

You have Resistance to Poison damage. You also have Advantage on saving throws you make to avoid or end the Poisoned condition.

Dwarven Toughness

Your Hit Point maximum increases by 1, and it increases by 1 again whenever you gain a level.

Stonecunning

As a Bonus Action, you gain Tremorsense with a range of 60 feet for 10 minutes. You must be on a stone surface or touching a stone surface to use this Tremorsense. The stone can be natural or worked. You can use this Bonus Action a number of times equal to your Proficiency Bonus, and you regain all expended uses when you finish a Long Rest.

Fey-touched

Reference: Half-Elf

Some people are born just slightly wrong: too fair, too still, ears that come to a point, eyes that catch the light oddly. They age slowly and live long — long enough to outlast everyone who remembers them arriving. The world calls them fey-touched, and not kindly.

Fey-touched are born to human parents, usually. Families occasionally whisper about an ancestor who "went into the woods for a year and came back changed." Whether that means anything, no one can say. There are old tales about a fair folk called "the elves" who may once have lived beyond the tree line — but tales are all they are, kept alive mostly within fey-touched family lines.

Whatever their truth, fey-touched live as outsiders: tolerated in towns, whispered about in villages, and occasionally turned out by frightened neighbors. Many grow thick skins. Many grow strange ones.

Humanoid · Medium (about 5–6 ft. tall) · Speed 30 ft.

"Minor" Magic Template (idea)

Involving access to Enchantment spells.

Gnome

Reference: Forest Gnome

Gnomes are a small, spry, long-lived folk of the deep woods — the "little people" of grandmother's warnings, though the warnings only sometimes have it right. They are real, but rarely seen: they live past the tree line, avoid roads, and deal with outsiders cautiously, if at all.

A gnome among humans is a genuine oddity. Country folk treat them with the mix of awe and dread reserved for the fair folk. Gnomes are watchful, practical, and stubbornly alive, which in this world suggests someone or something is looking out for them.

Humanoid · Small (about 3–4 ft. tall) · Speed 25 ft.

"Minor" Magic Template (idea)

Involving access to Illusion spells.

Greyborn (Goblin-Blooded)

Reference: Half-Orc

Greyborn are the green- or grey-skinned children of humans and goblin stock. Broad-shouldered, strong-jawed, hot of temper, they inherit their goblin side's hardiness and drive.

They are born and raised in the marches and borderlands, where the human realms grind against the Goblin people, and they belong fully to neither.

The world does not know what to do with them. The Church does not preach against them — but the Chancery keeps track of where they live, and when the goblin wars flare, neighbors remember whose face remind them of the enemy.

A Greyborn grows up knowing all of this. Some spend a lifetime trying to outrun their reputation, while others lean into the prejudice.

Humanoid · Medium (about 6–7 ft. tall) · Speed 30 ft.

Adrenaline Rush

You can take the Dash action as a Bonus Action. When you do so, you gain a number of Temporary Hit Points equal to your Proficiency Bonus. You can use this trait a number of times equal to your Proficiency Bonus, and you regain all expended uses when you finish a Short or Long Rest.

Relentless Endurance

When you are reduced to 0 Hit Points but not killed outright, you can drop to 1 Hit Point instead. Once you use this trait, you can't do so again until you finish a Long Rest.

Player Options

Classes

Magic in this world is rare, precious, and never free.

A player’s guide to the ways of life open to your character in the world of The Script for My Requiem. Most people live and die by the strength of their arms, the sharpness of their wits, or the depth of their faith. Choose what you are, and let the world decide what that makes you.

The Martial Paths

Fighter

The backbone of every army, watch, and caravan guard in the realm. Fighters study war the way clerics study scripture — with devotion. Where others pray or bargain, the fighter answers with steel, discipline, and ten thousand hours of practice.

  • Champion — The natural. Some fighters simply are the weapon: perfect strikes, tireless lungs, the kind of talent bards write songs about.
  • Battle Master — The scholar of war. Every battlefield is a text; every foe, a sentence with a weakness in its grammar. Maneuvers learned, studied, executed.
  • Cavalier — The rider, the bulwark. Born in the saddle, sworn to hold the line. Protects what stands behind them — or rides it down.

Rogue

The world is not kind, and rogues learned that early. They are the shadow-economists of civilization: lockpicks, silver tongues, and knives where you didn’t expect them. Not all are criminals — some are investigators, scouts, duelists, spies. All of them see the angles.

  • Thief — The classic. Fast hands, faster feet, and an uncanny instinct for the exact moment no one is watching.
  • Assassin — Death as a trade. Studied, patient, precise. The mark never hears the second step.
  • Mastermind — The puppeteer. Fights with words, plans, and other people. By the time you realize you’ve lost, you lost a week ago.
  • Inquisitive — The bloodhound. Reads a room like scripture: the splintered door, the nervous tic, the lie shaped exactly like the truth.
  • Scout — The pathfinder. First into the dark, last to be seen. Survives where maps end.
  • Swashbuckler — The duelist. A blade, a grin, and footwork that makes death look like dancing.

Barbarian

Civilization is a thin crust, and barbarians remember what walks beneath it. Their fury is not technique — it is something older, and it comes out of the marrow, not the mind.

  • Path of the Berserker — Fury without brakes. The body as a storm; the enemy as the shore.
  • Path of the Battlerager — The dwarf-tradition of spiked armor and utter madness: hurt me, and I will hurt you twice.
  • Path of the Totem Warrior — The old ways. A spirit watches over you — bear, eagle, wolf. The village grandmother would call it a kind of faith. The Church has opinions on that. (Rituals, instincts, and things spoken over campfires at the edge of the Weald.)

The Old Paths — Primal Ways

Druid

The world remembers what it was before walls and wards, and druids are the ones who still listen. Their magic is not granted by any altar, cannot be revoked by any bishopric, and needs no license — which is precisely what makes both churches nervous. Circles keep the old balances: growth and rot, storm and stillness.

  • Circle of the Land — The steward. Warded by the land itself — the grove, the swamp, the mountain — and drawing from its memory.
  • Circle of the Moon — The shapeshifter. Wears the wild from the inside. In animal form, druids of this circle are not mimicking the beast — they are borrowing the world’s own shape.
  • Circle of Dreams — The fey-touched keeper. Mists, twilit charms, and the knowledge that the old folk of the woods are real, and can be kind, on their terms.
  • Circle of the Shepherd — The spirit-herd. Calls the unseen fauna of the wild — things with antlers of smoke and breath like fog — and they answer.
  • Circle of Spores — The caretaker of the last cycle. Fungus, rot, the beautiful ugliness of decay. Not death: recycling. The circle the Church likes least and understands least.

Ranger

Half fighter, half druid, entirely at home where the roads end. A ranger’s magic is small, instinctive, and tangled up with survival itself — the way a hawk knows thermals, a ranger knows the wild.

  • Hunter — The specialist of monsters. Knows what hunts in the dark, and how to hunt it back.
  • Beast Master — The bonded. Shares a mind and a heartbeat with a true companion — a bond older than marriage and harder to break.
  • Gloom Stalker — The midnight scout. Ambush, dread, and eyes that pierce the dark — often the first to see what should have stayed unseen.
  • Fey Wanderer — The wanderer between worlds. Charming, unsettling, and never entirely sure which side of the hedge they woke up on.
  • Swarmkeeper — The accompanied. Followed by a swarm that is more than insects — spirits of the small and the many. They do not command it; they walk with it.

The Faithful — Divine Paths

Cleric

The Light’s answer to a dark world. Every cleric is an instrument of the Dawnward — healer, judge, soldier of the faith. Ordination is not a formality; it is a contract witnessed by heaven, and the Church keeps the paperwork.

  • Light Domain — Radiance, truth, the fire of the sun. The Light made weapon and witness. The most public face of the faith.
  • War Domain — The Church militant. Consecrated steel, blessed wrath, crusade-magic. The Light does not turn the other cheek at the marches.
  • Knowledge Domain — The archivists and inquisitors of truth. The Church remembers everything — and a Knowledge cleric is the memory itself.
  • Grave Domain — The keepers of the boundary. Last rites, sealed tombs, and the fierce mercy of keeping the dead dead. Nobody performs more good and gets less thanks.

A word on the dwarves: The mountain folk keep their own ways — the forge-faith, smith-gods, steel blessed in fire. The Church tolerates this the way one tolerates an old uncle’s opinions: never endorsed, never challenged. If you are of dwarven blood, the Forge Domain answers to your altar, not ours.

Whispers — The Other Paths

Every village has a story about someone who made a bargain. The stories don’t have happy endings, but they don’t always have sad ones, either. There is no class for this. If it happens to your character, it will be because of something you did — a door you knocked on, a promise you made. Ask your GM no questions; the answers cost more than the asking.

Speak with your GM about which of these paths fits the story you want to tell — and remember: in this world, power always has a source, and the source always has terms.

Concept Work

Redesigning the Magic System

Working Document · Work in Progress

From time to time, the foundations of the world are taken apart and rebuilt. This is one of those workings: a redesign of the magic system — and, with it, of the character classes that use magic. What follows is an open look at the thinking in progress; nothing here is final until it appears elsewhere on this site.

Premise

  • Magic is not a commodity, not some whimsical "everyone does it" thing.
  • Magic should have weight, feel impactful and precious, and needs to be used carefully and purposefully.
  • We do away with easy-to-use combat magic. No more fireballs — and specifically no spamable combat cantrips like Fire Bolt or Ray of Frost or whatever.
    • If used in combat, magic should be buffs or debuffs, maybe healing magic, or the manipulation of the environment.
    • In general, magic should be manipulating the world or, in some cases, a creature or person — but not instantly causing something out of thin air.
  • Since classes like Wizards and Sorcerers are outlawed, and subclasses that use arcane magic like the Eldritch Knight are done away with, we want to see where arcane magic can be put back into the world and made meaningful.

The Classes

  • Wizards, Sorcerers, Warlocks and Bards do not exist as player options, at least not in the sense of D&D character classes.
  • Same goes for subclasses of other classes — e.g. the Arcane Trickster, the Eldritch Knight, and what else there is.
  • The Magic Templates idea:
    • Warlocks are not classes anymore. A Warlock instead becomes a story/world-building term for someone who works in service of a higher being that grants them certain powers.
    • To address something like that, there will be Magic Templates that can be attached to a character. These are additional character features that involve magic use, access to certain spells (or a full spell school), and some additional abilities for the character.
    • Becoming a warlock grants a selected Magic Template, based on the patron chosen and individual selection.
    • A part of each Magic Template would be the learning of one spell school (see further down the line).
    • There might be other reasons to attach such a Magic Template to a character. Maybe all Forest Gnomes are given a certain Magic Template that grants access to the illusion spell school or something — thus a forest gnome rogue could become a kind of Arcane Trickster again (a rogue that can use illusion magic).
    • Bards will become Warlocks, basically. Bard will be one, or several, of the Magic Template types for the warlock template, granting special abilities revolving around performance.
    • An example Magic Template could grant some bard abilities (directly copied from the bard class in D&D) and access to the enchantment school.
    • Another example could be the arcane trickster, granting access to the illusion school and some other rogue abilities, maybe from the arcane trickster subclass of D&D itself.
    • Another example could be a fighter getting into a pact with some entity, granting them some Paladin- or Hexblade-like abilities and access to the transmutation school to buff themselves (with Haste, Enlargement, etc.).

Open question: Should ancestries come with magical powers and, if yes: how is that balanced between other ancestries?

Magic Types and Spell Schools

Arcane Magic

The magic used, in D&D 5E, by Bards, Wizards, Warlocks, Sorcerers, and several subclasses of martial classes, like the Eldritch Knight or the Arcane Trickster. Main focus for now.

Arcane magic is actually the biggest offender — specifically the Evocation spell school. The idea is to just go so far as to do away with all the Evocation spells. Other schools, however, are more interesting.

  • Strip away Evocation.
  • Illusion, Enchantment, Transmutation, Divination are all very interesting, and we want to weave them in somewhere.
  • Abjuration is a "maybe". Perhaps some of the spells can be put into other schools, depending on what they actually abjure.
  • Conjuration: yes and no. There is an argument for summoning demons or whatever, but this should only be doable in a ritual sense. Conjuring a hammer out of nowhere, though, is nonsense.
  • Necromancy is much the same as with Conjuration. The idea of bringing the dead back to life is awesome, talking to the dead is amazing — even something like traversing to a "Plane of the Dead" would be amazing. However, spamable damage spells that happen to have the Necromancy school attached feels cheap.
  • Besides all of this, even for schools we want to keep in, we have to look carefully at spells that make sense, and spells we don't want in our campaign.
  • We will have to do that for divine magic and primal magic as well, by the way — but that is for another time.

Divine Magic

The magic used by Paladins and Clerics.

  • Untouched by now; we look at this later.
  • At least there will be some stripping of spells and abilities of certain classes and spell schools.

Primal Magic

The magic used by Druids and Rangers.

  • Untouched by now; we look at this later.
  • At least there will be some stripping of spells and abilities of certain classes and spell schools.

Spell School Categories: Inherent, Extrinsic

Two categories for what we are dealing with:

  • Inherent: Spell schools we want to put into Magic Templates, so a character could gain access to them.
    • Where this magic precisely comes from might be differing.
      • A pact with some entity — pretty much the Warlock route.
      • Maybe some angel or even a god itself might grant this power to their chosen (independently of being a priest or cleric).
        • Maybe we do away with the priest/cleric classes in the first place and also make that just templates?
          • ...and leave the only true "magic class" to be a druid.
      • Maybe it comes from being the descendant of fey folk.
        • So maybe it's attached to the Forest Gnome and/or Fey-Touched ancestries.
  • Extrinsic: Magic we want to exist in the world, but rather cast in longer rituals, out of a book.
    • This magic can (maybe) be used by basically everyone smart enough, as long as they have the book and can read it to follow its instructions.
    • This will still be power invoked in some way or form from entities. Most old tomes of magic will probably hold knowledge that's been left by the old gods or something — old forgotten rituals.

Based on the premises and thoughts above, the schools of Illusion, Enchantment, Transmutation and Divination fall under the Inherent category, while Conjuration, Necromancy and possibly Abjuration fall under the Extrinsic category.

The Balancing Act

Open question: How do we balance the power level of characters with a Magic Template attached — for example, by a Warlock pact — against characters that haven't?

  • Does it need balance, or is it just fine to have some characters be more powerful?
  • Do we simply make the drawbacks attached to "becoming a warlock" weigh enough to justify an increased power level?
  • What do we do with other concepts, like attaching a Magic Template to a certain ancestry? Is that a good idea? What would the drawbacks have to be?
